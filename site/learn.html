which one when

When two how-tos look alike, this tells them apart.

Something has to happen later
  • A whole job that starts laterRun a job once, later
  • A pause inside a job that has already begunWait for time to pass without a running process
  • An exact instant instead of a delaySchedule, sleep, and reschedule to an exact instant
Work must not happen twice
  • Work that finished must not repeat on a retryKeep finished work from repeating on a retry
  • A side effect where a second attempt is worse than not knowing, such as capturing a paymentRefuse to repeat a side effect after a crash
  • A repeated request must not create a second jobKeep a repeated request from creating a second job
A job has to wait
  • For a person or another system that tells youWait for an approval without holding a worker
  • For time to passWait for time to pass without a running process
  • For an outside system you have to ask againCheck again later without spending a retry
Work repeats on a schedule
  • One job the schedule fires again and againRun a job every day at a set hour in your time zone
  • Every occurrence a job of its own, with its own retries and resultMake every occurrence its own job
  • One job that keeps its place between firingsKeep another system in sync with a permanent job
One job becomes many
  • Children from a list, their results joinedFan out over a list and merge the results
  • Different children, or enqueue now and wait laterFan out to child jobs with Map or WaitChildren
  • Independent jobs with nothing to joinEnqueue many independent jobs from a handler

Take the first steps

Define a job, start it, and read back what it did. Start at the top.

  1. 1Run your first jobOne [Job] method, one enqueue, and a worker that runs it
  2. 2Pass typed input to a jobThe input is a record: Acta stores it with the job and hands it to the handler as its first parameter
  3. 3Return a result and wait for itWhat the handler returns is stored with the job as its result. WaitAsync<T> follows the job to its end and hands the result back, typed
  4. 4Use your own services in a handlerThe class that holds the handler takes its services in its constructor, from the application's own container
  5. 5Read a job's statusGetAsync answers a snapshot of the job: its ref, its name and namespace, its status, and when it was created
  6. 6Read a job's resultTryGetResultAsync<T> finds nothing before Succeeded; GetResultAsync<T> returns the result you expect
  7. 7Start a job from an HTTP endpoint and poll itA POST stores the job and answers 202 with its ref; a GET reads the status and, once the job has finished, the result
  8. 8Build the first-job guide's appThe first-job guide's app verbatim: typed result, a failure that is final and one that retries, explanation, cancel, and a wait that times out without stopping the job

Start jobs

Every way to start work: now, later, once only, by name, or many at a time.

  1. 1Enqueue by the input's typeEnqueueAsync takes the input alone: its type names the job, so there is no name string to get wrong
  2. 2Enqueue by the job's generated callThe manifest's generated call names the job and takes its handler's parameters, several of them as one input
  3. 3Enqueue a job by its nameAn EnqueueJobRequest names the namespace and the job as strings, for a caller that does not have the job's types
  4. 4Write a job that takes no inputA job with no input: a handler with no parameters, enqueued by its generated call
  5. 5Pass one plain value as the inputThe input can be one plain value, such as a string or a number, with no record around it
  6. 6Keep several jobs in one classA class can hold several [Job] methods. Each is a job of its own, with its own generated call
  7. 7Run a job once, laterScheduleAsync(input, delay) stores the job now and makes it due after the delay. No process has to stay alive until then
  8. 8Schedule, sleep, and reschedule to an exact instantPin work to an instant instead of a delay: ScheduleAsync(input, runAt) starts a job then, SleepUntilAsync pauses a handler until then, and RescheduleUntilAsync runs the handler again then
  9. 9Keep a repeated request from creating a second jobA deduplication key makes an enqueue idempotent: a second enqueue with the same key answers the job that is already there
  10. 10Allow one job per hour or per dayA deduplication key built from a time bucket lets one job in per bucket: DeduplicationKey.PerTimeBucket here, with PerHour and PerDay as its fixed sizes
  11. 11Find a job by its deduplication keyJobLookup.ByDeduplicationKey finds a job by the key it was enqueued with, when you did not keep its ref
  12. 12Choose between starting a job and waiting for itEnqueueAsync returns as soon as the job is stored; CallAsync enqueues, waits, and returns the result
  13. 13Enqueue many jobs in one round tripEnqueueJobsAsync writes a whole list of jobs in one round trip to the database and answers their outcomes in request order
  14. 14Run urgent jobs firstA priority on the enqueue decides which of the jobs that are due at once a worker claims first
  15. 15Label jobs so you can find themTags are name and value labels set on the enqueue, for filtering and grouping jobs in queries and in the dashboard
  16. 16Pass a large file by referenceKeep a big file in blob storage and enqueue a reference to it, with its checksum and size, instead of the bytes
  17. 17Choose a payload format for a jobA job names the format its payload is stored in. This program runs custom formats side by side and compares their serialization time, enqueue time, readability, and stored bytesLab

Run work on a schedule

Recurring work as one permanent job: by cron, by interval, in a time zone, or from a calendar.

  1. 1Run a job on a repeating schedule[Schedule] with a cron makes one permanent job that fires on that cadence, with nothing to enqueue
  2. 2Run a job every day at a set hour in your time zoneA cron with TimeZoneId fires at the same local hour all year: 0 8 * * * in Europe/Berlin is 08:00 in Berlin, in summer and in winter
  3. 3Replace a timer loop with an interval scheduleAn interval schedule, such as 10s, runs a job at a fixed gap, where a timer loop in a hosted service would otherwise stand
  4. 4Give one job several schedulesSeveral [Schedule] attributes on one job share one permanent job, and schedules that come due together fire it onceLab
  5. 5Decide what happens to runs a schedule missedMissed occurrences are a business decision. Three schedules miss the same stretch while paused, and each one's misfire policy decides it differently: Skip runs at most the latest, CatchUpOnce runs one firing that covers them all, CatchUpAll runs every one in orderLab
  6. 6Pause and resume a scheduleoperations.Schedules pauses a schedule from outside the handler, for good or until an instant, and resumes it
  7. 7Drive a schedule from a published calendarA calendar is data that an operator publishes through operations.Calendars. A schedule binds it by name, and a later publication retimes every schedule bound to it in one transaction while workers run
  8. 8Make every occurrence its own jobScheduledDelivery = PerOccurrence makes each occurrence a root job of its own, with its own ref, retries, result, and retention. MaxOutstanding bounds how many may be unfinished at once
  9. 9Decide what a failed firing does to its scheduleA firing that spends its retry budget returns the permanent job to Idle. FiringFailurePolicy says what happens to its schedules then: Continue, the default, fires the next occurrence as usual, and PauseScheduling pauses them, with the reason, until an operator resumes them
  10. 10Start each firing of a recurring job cleanA permanent job keeps its variables from one firing to the next. ResetStateAsync at the end of a firing clears them, so the next one starts blank

Keep another system in sync

One job that keeps calling a service and remembers how far it got.

  1. 1Keep another system in sync with a permanent jobOne permanent job calls a service on a schedule and keeps a watermark of how far it got. Each firing syncs one bounded batch and returns; the watermark is the job's own durable variable, read at the top of every execution, so a recovered firing continues where the lost one stoppedLab
  2. 2Keep a value across retriesA durable variable stores a value with the job the first time it is computed, and every later attempt reads the same value
  3. 3Check again later without spending a retryRescheduleAsync ends the execution and runs the handler again after a delay, without counting as a failure or spending a retry

Make work survive a crash

Steps and variables record what a handler finished, so a retry or a restart carries on instead of starting over.

  1. 1Keep finished work from repeating on a retryA named step records its outcome, so a retry returns it instead of doing the work againLab
  2. 2Feed one step's result into the nextA step can return a value. Each result is recorded once, so the next step takes it as its input and a replay rebuilds the chain from the records
  3. 3Compute a value once and reuse itGetOrSetVariableAsync computes a value on its first call and stores it with the job; later calls return the stored value. The program also checks for a variable, deletes it, reads it with a default, and stores a raw JobPayload
  4. 4Compensate when the last attempt failsA failing step retries through its job, and a catch on EndsJob compensates before the failure ends the job
  5. 5Refuse to repeat a side effect after a crashAn at-most-once step refuses to run its body a second time after a crash. The handler is told that the outcome is unknown, and reconcilesLab
  6. 6Build a checkout that waits for a review and a timerOne handler reads top to bottom as a checkout: wait for a fraud review, reserve, charge, wait out a settlement window, send the receipt. Each stage is a signal, a step, a variable, or a timer that survives a restartLab
  7. 7Report how far along a job isSetProgressAsync stores how far the job has come, where a status page or the dashboard reads it while the job runs

Wait for people, events, and time

A waiting job is a row. No worker and no process stays busy while it waits.

  1. 1Wait for an approval without holding a workerA job parks on a named signal, holds no worker, and carries on when the signal is raisedLab
  2. 2Wait for time to pass without a running processA named sleep stores the due time, frees the worker, and wakes the job when the time comesLab
  3. 3Read the outcome of a wait that can time outCallAsync answers a JobOutcome: whether the wait timed out, whether the job failed, and the value when it succeeded

Split work across child jobs

Fan out, join the results, and decide what a failed child means.

  1. 1Start child jobs and wait for themA parent starts child jobs by name, suspends without holding a worker until every child has finished, and reads their resultsLab
  2. 2Call another job and use its resultCallChildAsync starts another job as a child, waits for it without holding a worker, and returns its result
  3. 3Fan out over a list and merge the resultsMapAsync starts one child job per item of a list, waits for them all, and hands back each item's result for the parent to merge
  4. 4Fan out to child jobs with Map or WaitChildrenTwo ways to fan out: EnqueueChildAsync with WaitChildrenAsync for a few named children, and MapAsync for one child per item of a list
  5. 5Handle child jobs that failA group of children never throws by itself. A failed child shows in the Failed list of the wait or the map, and ThrowIfAnyFailed is how the parent chooses to fail with it
  6. 6Start a child job in another namespaceA parent starts a child in another worker's namespace and waits across the boundary
  7. 7Enqueue many independent jobs from a handlerA handler takes IJobs and enqueues many independent jobs, each with a stable deduplication key. Nothing links them to the handler's job and nothing waits for them (compare 212's MapAsync)
  8. 8Chain jobs into a pipelineEach stage is a job of its own that enqueues the next one, with a deduplication key so a replayed handoff starts no second pipeline

Decide what happens when work fails

Retries, backoff, timeouts, deadlines, and ending a job on purpose.

  1. 1Limit how many times a job retriesMaxAttempts on the job is its retry budget. When the last attempt fails, the job ends as Failed
  2. 2Set how long a job waits between retriesBackoff on the job sets how the wait between retries grows: 1s..8s x2 exact starts at one second, doubles with each failure, and stops growing at eight
  3. 3Fail a job for good, with no retryFailAsync ends the job as Failed at once, whatever its retry budget. A thrown exception would be retried
  4. 4Cancel a job from inside its handlerCancelAsync in a handler ends the job as Cancelled: terminal, not a failure, so no retry and no alert follow
  5. 5Pause a job from inside its handlerPauseAsync in a handler parks the job as Paused until someone resumes it
  6. 6Cut off an attempt that runs too longExecutionTimeout caps one attempt: when the time is up, the handler's CancellationToken trips and the attempt ends as a timeout
  7. 7Stop cleanly when a job is cancelledA handler takes a CancellationToken and passes it to everything it awaits, so a shutdown or a cancel ends it promptly
  8. 8Drop a job that is past its deadlineA Deadline is the whole job's budget, measured from its creation. Under the default, strict behaviour an overdue job is cancelled before its handler runs
  9. 9Run an overdue job in a reduced modeWith DeadlineBehavior = Advisory an overdue job still runs. The handler reads IsOverdue and chooses a reduced path

Control order and load

Keep jobs that touch the same thing from running over each other.

  1. 1Let one job at a time touch a resourceRunWithLockAsync holds a named lock around a piece of work, so jobs that name the same lock take turns
  2. 2Limit how many jobs run at once for one keyA ConcurrencyKey on the enqueue lets one job at a time run for that key. ConcurrencyLimit on the job gives a key several slots, and RateLimit meters how often jobs startLab
See order, limits, and pacing move in the interactive explainer

Watch and control running work

Read what happened, find jobs, and cancel, pause, resume, or restart them.

  1. 1Inspect a job from the command lineThe app's own binary answers jobs info, events, explain, and the control verbs for any job, read from the same databaseLab
  2. 2See your jobs in the dashboardMapActa serves the dashboard and its JSON API from your own app. Its controls are off until you turn them onLab
  3. 3Read why a job did what it didThe append-only event ledger answers why a job did what it did. ListEventsAsync with LineageRoot reads the timeline of a job and of every child it started
  4. 4List and page through jobsoperations.Ledger lists jobs with filters, pages through them by cursor, counts them, and answers the health counters an operator's screen opens with
  5. 5Cancel a running job from outsideCancelAsync on a job's handle cancels it from outside its handler. Any process that holds an IJobs can do it, and a running handler's CancellationToken trips
  6. 6Pause and resume a job from outsideIJobs.PauseAsync and ResumeAsync pause and resume a job from outside its handler. Each answers whether it was applied or rejected, and its reason lands on the job's timeline
  7. 7Restart a failed job and keep its historyRestartAsync runs a finished job again under the same identity. The failure budget is reset, the history is appended to, steps that succeeded are not run again, and the bare lines of the handler areLab
  8. 8Control jobs over HTTPMapActaApi mounts the operator API without the dashboard. Its controls are off until EnableControls is set, answer local requests only by default, and a control request has to confirm itself
  9. 9Require permissions on the HTTP APIRequirePermissions has every route of the HTTP API check the host's policy named for its permission: read, enqueue, control, or admin. A caller with no credential gets 401, and one that lacks the permission gets 403 naming it
  10. 10Put the dashboard behind your sign-inThe dashboard and its API sit behind the host's own sign-in. The host maps its roles to Acta's four permissions; with no session a page goes to the sign-in, and an API call answers 401
  11. 11Record which tenant a job is forTenantName on the enqueue records which tenant a job is for. Its children inherit the tenant, the job list filters by it, and an enqueue for an unknown or a suspended tenant is refusedLab
  12. 12Carry a correlation key from parent to childA CorrelationKey set on the enqueue travels with the job and with every child it starts. It is on the job row, in each handler's log scope, and a filter of the job list
  13. 13Wrap every handler in your own middlewareAn IJobPipelineBehavior wraps every handler call, the way middleware wraps a request
  14. 14Choose how many events a job recordsEventRecording on the job decides which events it writes. Audit, the default, records every event. Failures records the failed and orphaned attempts, the success that recovers from a failure, what an enabled alert profile reads, a caller's controls of the job, and a schedule's pauses and manual triggers

Get told when something breaks

Alerts raised by failures or by your own code, routed where your team looks.

  1. 1Get an alert when a job failsA failing job raises an alert by itself, with no AlertAsync in the handler. AlertProfile on the job says when, and repeats of the same job's failure count up on one alert rather than opening new ones
  2. 2Raise an alert from a handlerAlertAsync in a handler raises an alert for the people who operate the system, and operations.Alerts reads it back
  3. 3Send alerts to your own channelAddAlertChannel declares where alerts go, under a name of your choice, and an alert names the channel it is for
  4. 4Route alerts to Slack from configurationAn alert channel posts to a Slack webhook whose URL comes from configuration, and logs when none is set. A deduplication key collapses repeats of one incident into one alert
  5. 5Escalate an alert as failures add upAutomatic failure alerts escalate in stages: the first failure, the failure count reaching AlertFailureThreshold, and the final failure when the retry budget is spent

Test without sleeps or polling

The real runtime on a throwaway database, driven one tick at a time.

  1. 1Test a handler with the test hostActa.Testing runs the real runtime on a real database with no background loop: RunOnceAsync decides when a job executes, so a test drives one attempt at a time. Run it with dotnet test concepts/800-testing/801-testing-jobs
  2. 2Test a job that waits for a signalEach RunOnceAsync drives exactly one attempt, so the suspend on a signal and the resume after its raise are two explicit ticks, and the test asserts that the step before the wait ran once across the replay. Run it with dotnet test concepts/800-testing/802-testing-durable-jobs
  3. 3Test the first-job guide's appThe first-job guide's test verbatim: the real runtime on a throwaway SQLite file, driven one tick at a time, with a retry made due at once
  4. 4Test a recurring synchronization jobThe claims of the continuous-synchronization lab as tests: a schedule forced due and one firing driven at a time, one job identity across firings, the watermark kept on the job, a lost attempt reclaimed with the next execution continuing at the watermark, and a replayed, a failed, and a backlogged batch. Run it with dotnet test

Deploy and scale

Split the API from the worker, add workers, and tune them.

  1. 1Enqueue from a process that runs no workerReference<TManifest>() lets a process enqueue a namespace's jobs by type without running a worker for it. Run<TManifest>() is the process that executes them
  2. 2Run two namespaces in one processEach Run starts a worker for one namespace. Two of them in one process share the process and the database, and never each other's jobs
  3. 3Recover the work of a killed workerA worker process is killed in the middle of a job, and another worker reclaims the job once its lease has lapsed. The program runs on PostgreSQL or SQL ServerLab
  4. 4Run jobs under Native AOTA source-generated JsonSerializerContext given to UseJsonPayloads serializes payloads with no runtime reflection, and JobPayload.Json(value, typeInfo) builds a raw payload the same way
  5. 5Wake workers across processes with RedisUseRedisWakeup announces each enqueue over Redis, so a worker in another process starts the job within milliseconds instead of at its next poll
  6. 6Set how many jobs a worker runs at onceMaxConcurrentExecutors caps how many jobs one worker runs at once, and SafetyPollInterval bounds how long an idle worker sleeps between looks at the database
