Choosing

When Acta is the right choice, and when another tool is.

If the tool you run today already makes background work dependable and easy to see into, keep it. Acta is for work that has become application state: its steps, waits, retries, and history must survive a crash and stay in the database the application already uses.

Start from the kind of work

A local loop where a missed tick is harmless
BackgroundService or PeriodicTimer. Nothing needs to be stored, so nothing more is needed.
A script or container the platform starts on a calendar
cron, systemd timers, Windows Task Scheduler, a Kubernetes CronJob, or a cloud timer. The platform owns the clock and the process.
Messages between services
A message broker or a streaming platform, for pub/sub, fan-out, and streams you replay. Acta can run the work a message starts, with the message id as its deduplication key.
Background work the application owns
Acta, when the work needs retries, durable state, a history, or an operator who can pause, restart, or signal it.
A long-running workflow in .NET
Acta: one C# handler with steps, child jobs, signals, and timers, and its state as rows in your database.
Workflows across several languages, or drawn in a designer
A workflow platform. Acta is .NET only and has no visual designer or BPMN.

If you already run one of these

Hangfirebackground job runner

Keep it when it already runs your jobs well: a widely used runner with recurring jobs, automatic retries, continuations, and a dashboard, on storage you know, with batches and throttling in its paid packages.

Consider Acta when progress inside a job has to survive a restart: named steps, signals, long waits, and child jobs in one handler, with order and limits held across every worker and the job's state in SQL views you can query.

Quartz.NETscheduler

Keep it when the calendar is the hard part: rich cron and calendar rules, misfire handling, and clustering you have already tuned. Its 4.x line adds a dashboard, continuations, and execution groups that cap work across a cluster.

Consider Acta when the job itself has state to keep: steps whose results survive a restart, long waits, child jobs, lineage, and one answer to why a job is where it is.

TickerQscheduler

Keep it when you want a source-generated scheduler with persistence, a dashboard, priorities, and, in its 5.x line, durable chains of jobs with parallel branches.

Consider Acta when one job should carry its own progress: checkpoints inside the handler, signals and timers it waits on, lanes and limits, recovery evidence, and operator intervention.

Workflow Coreworkflow library

Keep it when your workflows are graphs of steps, declared in fluent C#, JSON, or YAML, and it already persists them where you need.

Consider Acta when you would rather write the workflow as an ordinary C# handler, with named steps, waits, and child jobs as its durable points, and run it under the same controls, limits, and SQL views as every other job.

Temporalworkflow platform

Keep it when workers in several languages share one workflow, you want deterministic replay of its event history, or you already run its server or use its cloud service.

Consider Acta when the workflow is .NET, its state belongs in the SQL database the application already uses, and you would rather not run a separate workflow server.

Elsa Workflowsworkflow library

Keep it when you want workflows as data that can change without a deploy, built in code or drawn in its designer, hosted in your application or on their own.

Consider Acta when your team writes and reviews workflows as plain C# handlers, and wants its jobs, schedules, and workflows in one model.

Durable TaskDurable Functions and its SDKs

Keep it when you host on Azure Functions, or want a managed Azure service to hold your orchestrations' state. Durable Functions can also keep it in SQL Server.

Consider Acta when the state belongs in the application's own PostgreSQL, SQL Server, or SQLite database, wherever the application runs, and you want checkpoints you place yourself rather than orchestrator code that replays.

Wolverinemessaging framework

Keep it when your background work is message handling: a durable inbox and outbox, durable local queues, and scheduled messages, on a database you already run.

Consider Acta when the unit is a job with a life of its own: steps, waits, child jobs, a schedule, a history, and an operator who can pause, restart, or signal it.

RabbitMQ, Kafka, Service Bus, SQSmessage broker

Keep it for messaging between services, those in other languages included, with whatever pub/sub, fan-out, or replay your broker or streaming platform offers.

Add Acta beside it when the work a message starts needs retries, steps, and a history: the broker carries the message, and Acta runs the work. Without a broker, Acta runs background work on the database alone.

Each row describes the project as its own documentation did in November 2026: Quartz.NET at 4.x, and TickerQ at 5.x, then a preview.

Signs that Acta fits, and signs that it does not

Acta fits when
  • The work must survive restarts, deploys, and crashed workers.
  • Someone will ask what ran, what failed, and why a job is stuck.
  • Operators need to pause, resume, cancel, restart, or signal jobs.
  • One customer's or one order's jobs must run in order, one at a time.
  • PostgreSQL or SQL Server already runs beside the app, or the app is one process on SQLite.
Choose something else when
  • Losing a tick is harmless and nobody will ask what happened.
  • The work is host maintenance that the OS or the platform owns.
  • Workers in more than one language share the orchestration.
  • You want local variables to survive a crash without naming what to keep. Acta runs the handler again from the top and skips the finished steps.
  • A duplicate external side effect cannot be made idempotent, guarded, or reconciled.
