Order, limits & pacing

Choose which jobs run in order, how many run at once, and how often they start.

A lane decides order. A concurrency key or limit decides how many run at once. A rate limit decides how often they start. The gates are independent, so a job passes every one it carries, and a job that carries none is never slowed by them.

OrderLane

One job of the lane at a time, in enqueue order, like an SQS FIFO message group. The lane holds through retries.

o => o.Lane("customer-42")
How many at once · 1ConcurrencyKey

Never two at once per key. Order is not promised: a younger job may take a freed key first.

o => o.ConcurrencyKey("rebuild:acme")
How many at once · NConcurrencyLimit

The same gate with N slots, per key when the enqueue names one, otherwise for the whole definition.

[Job("rebuild-index", ConcurrencyLimit = 4)]
How oftenRateLimit

Starts metered on one clock across the namespace’s workers. Early arrivals are booked a turn and come back for it.

[Job("send-invoice", RateLimit = "10/s")]

Measured, on one rig

32 logical cores, NVMe, zero-work handlers, 8 executors, on the rc.5 benchmark round. One lane holding a thousand jobs ran about 230 to 260 jobs a second on PostgreSQL and SQL Server, each handoff waiting for the previous job's completion to commit. A thousand lanes in round-robin ran at 80 to 99 percent of unlaned throughput. One hot concurrency key with limit 1 managed 16 to 19 jobs a second on the servers, because every loser is claimed, bounced, and re-armed. Rough illustrations of the shape of each gate, not capacity claims. Benchmark round

Certified, under chaos

The release certification kills a worker every five seconds while laned jobs run, then checks from the database's own ledger that no lane ever ran two members at once or out of order, and that every lane drained. The same run checks three rate meters, at 10, 50 and 100 starts a second, never admitted past their published budget. Certification records
