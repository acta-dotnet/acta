How Acta works

Everything is a job, and every job is a row.

Acta is a library in your .NET process and tables in your database. Everything that has to outlive a request is a job: a row a worker claims under a lease, retries under a budget, and records in a ledger. Steps, waits, signals, and progress are durable state on that row. Children are more rows, with lineage. A schedule makes one row claimable again and again. One model, one engine, one set of views, and nothing else to run.

The model in one figure

A permanent job, through one firing.

A definition with a [Schedule] owns one durable slot. Admission makes the slot claimable when a schedule is due, a worker runs the handler for a bounded amount of work, and the slot returns to Idle holding no thread. Acta's progress and the job's are two different rows, and they never touch.

Acta's progress

The schedule cursor says when the slot is next due; the firing envelope says which schedules admitted this firing and how it settled. Admission writes both in one apply, so an occurrence is admitted once across every worker.

The job's progress

The watermark is a variable on the job, written by the handler after each batch and read first on every execution. Acta keeps it the way it keeps the job: a row in checkpoints_view, carried across firings, retries, and workers. It is where the next firing starts, and where a recovered firing continues after a crash.

Why they never touch

A firing that finds nothing to sync still moves the cursor. A manual trigger moves the watermark and not the cursor. Each kind of progress answers one question, and neither has to be reconciled with the other.

What is a job?the unit

The only thing a worker claims. A job is a row with a name, an input, a status, a lease, a retry budget, and a ledger of events. A worker claims it under the lease, runs your handler, and writes what happened. Nothing else is claimed on its own.

One durable work unit
Definition, or instance?two things named job

A definition is the [Job] on a handler: the durable name, the retry policy, the schedules. An instance is a row made from it, enqueued by your code, admitted by a schedule, or started by a parent. One definition, any number of rows, each with its own public handle.

What are steps and checkpoints?state on the row

Durable state that belongs to a job. A step records its outcome, so a re-run returns the stored result instead of repeating the work; variables, timers, signals, and progress are checkpoints on the same job. They share the job's claim, lease, retry budget, and events, and are never claimed on their own.

Durable slots inside a job
What are parents and children?more rows

Ordinary jobs. A parent starts children through Map, Parallel, or Join; each child is a row with the parent's lineage, its own retries, and its own result, and the parent waits on a durable latch without holding a worker. Fan-out is more jobs, not another kind of thing.

What is a permanent job?one slot, fired again and again

A definition with a [Schedule]: one durable slot the schedule fires again and again. The slot is Idle between firings and holds no thread; admission makes it Ready when a schedule is due, a worker claims it, and the handler does a bounded amount of work and returns. Its steps and progress persist across firings, and the handler may reset them as its final action. This is how a synchronization controller runs without a loop that must never die.

What is per-occurrence delivery?a job per occurrence

The other mode for a schedule. Each occurrence becomes a fresh ordinary root job with its own state, retries, result, and retention, and MaxOutstanding bounds how many of one schedule's roots may be unsettled at once. Use it when each period is its own business event: a statement run, an import whose files must each be tracked. Backfilling past periods is application work under either mode.

Scheduler, or executor?two passes, no coordinator

Two passes over the same rows, on every worker. Admission turns a due schedule into a firing: it moves the cursor and writes the firing envelope in one apply, so an occurrence is admitted once however many workers run. Claiming takes Ready rows under a lease and runs them. A schedule never runs code; it only makes a row claimable.

What survives a crash?everything that committed

The row, its status, the lease, the firing envelope, every recorded step, every checkpoint, and every event are in your database. The dead worker's lease runs out, a heartbeat on any worker reclaims the row, and the next execution re-enters the handler from the top, where the recorded steps and your own committed progress say where to continue. Where does at-least-once end?at your side effects

A step's recorded outcome is repeat-safe, but your writes and Acta's record of them commit separately, so a handler can run again for work that already landed. An idempotency key, an upsert, or a watermark written after the work makes that harmless. AtMostOnce() trades a possible repeat for an explicitly ambiguous outcome.

Why no workflow engine?composition, not a runtime

Because a workflow is a composition of jobs and their durable state, not a second runtime. A parent, its children, a long wait, a permanent controller: the same claim, lease, recovery, retry budget, dashboard, CLI, and SQL views serve all of them. There is nothing new to deploy or operate, and no determinism contract on your code.
