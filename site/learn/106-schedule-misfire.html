The lab behind it: a missed schedule is a business decision

This program is an Engineering Lab: beside the how-to it prints the rows that show what happened, and its notes weigh the design against the alternatives.

The problem

After downtime or a pause, a schedule cursor points into the past. "Run everything missed," "run once now," and "skip ahead" have different business meanings and operational costs.

Common approaches

  • Skip missed instants and move to the next future occurrence.
  • Catch up once, coalescing the outage into one recovery execution.
  • Run every missed occurrence, in order, as its own execution.
  • Materialize every occurrence as independently tracked work.

Why this design

Each schedule names its policy, and Skip is the default when it names none. A resume, like a deploy or a worker start, decides nothing: it keeps the cursor where it was, and the next admission pass applies the policy to the occurrences it finds already past. An occurrence less than a minute late still counts as on time.

  • Skip skips the missed occurrences, except the latest when it is within that minute.
  • CatchUpOnce runs one firing whose occurrence is the earliest missed and whose covered-through is the latest, so the handler knows the stretch it stands for.
  • CatchUpLatest runs the latest however late it is and skips the earlier ones.
  • CatchUpAll runs every missed occurrence in order, one firing per settlement.

[Schedule(ExpireAfter = "1h", ExpireAction = ...)] bounds how old an occurrence may be before any of these apply: older ones are skipped with the reason expired, and Pause also pauses the schedule.

Trade-offs

Skip loses missed work. CatchUpOnce keeps the stretch but not one execution per occurrence. CatchUpAll keeps every occurrence but runs them one after another on one permanent job, so a long outage becomes a long backlog; with ScheduledDelivery = PerOccurrence each one is a root job of its own.

Run the experiment

The program pauses three schedules that fire every second, lets about three occurrences pass, resumes them, and waits until each policy has decided the stretch. It prints how many firings each ran for the missed occurrences: skip-report at most one, catch-up-once-report one covering the stretch, and catch-up-all-report one per missed occurrence, in order.

Rows to inspect

Run with --all-columns to execute the visible SELECT * Explore query first. The default Notice queries select the fields that prove the lesson, and the text below explains their meaning.

schedules_view shows the three cursors in the past while the schedules are paused. After the resume, events_view shows each policy's first decision: a schedule.skipped range for skip-report beside its admission of the latest, one schedule.admitted for catch-up-once-report whose details carry the covered-through instant, and the first of catch-up-all-report's admissions.

Break it

Pause these one-second schedules for as long as you like: Skip still runs the latest occurrence, since one fell due less than a second before the resume and the grace is a minute. Give a schedule occurrences more than a minute apart and resume it more than a minute after the one it missed, and Skip runs none of them, while CatchUpLatest would still run that latest one.

When not to use

Use Skip for cache refresh, telemetry, and cleanup. Use CatchUpOnce for reconciliation or summary work where one recovery pass matters. Use CatchUpAll, or a per-occurrence definition, for accounting or financial occurrences that must never coalesce.
